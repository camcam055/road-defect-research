1.0 Introduction

1.1 Background

Flexible pavements are a fundamental component of transport infrastructure. They typically consist of four layers; subgrade, subbase, base and wearing surface.

In New Zealand, the road network plays a critical role in supporting the country’s economy. This is because roads in New Zealand are used to transport goods up and down the country, which is essential for maintaining efficient supply chains and a stable economy. Because of this, having high-quality roads that maintain high structural performance is essential for supporting the country.

New Zealand’s economy is expanding at a steady rate, which is increasing the volume of traffic moving up and down the country. According to Waka Kotahi NZ Transport Agency, New Zealand has over 24,500 lane kilometres of road that need to be maintained. This highlights the scale and complex of the national network. There are portions of the current road infrastructure that do not align with the needs of the country.

However, many roads in New Zealand lack proper care, and many of these roads were constructed decades ago and the roads now were not built to accommodate current traffic volumes and the heavier vehicle loads. As a result, the pavement structure experiences a higher level of stress and deterioration, which then increases the need for an effective pavement monitoring and maintenance strategy.

Modern vehicles, increased freight movement and an overall higher traffic volume place greater stress on the outdated roads and the pavement structure. This is accelerating pavement deterioration, while maintenance and rehabilitation are unable to keep up with the rate of deterioration.

Because deterioration of these roads is speeding up, it is important to have an effective maintenance strategy for finding defects on New Zealand’s roads. Early identification of surface defects such as potholes, cracking and alligator cracking is critical for preventing further road damage. Currently, finding these surface defects in the early stage of development is done manually. In New Zealand, there are 23 core contracts across the country that support the maintenance of the road infrastructure. The primary way surface defects are identified is through manual visual inspections which are carried out by road maintenance inspection teams. Given the scale of the road network, early-stage defects can be easily missed, allowing the small defects to develop into more serious structural damage.

The approach aimed to reduce manual inspection effort, improve detection accuracy, and provide a cost-effective method for detecting early-stage defects and improving maintenance decision-making.

1.2 Problem Statement

New Zealand’s road network spans over 24,500 lane kilometres and plays a vital role in supporting New Zealand’s economy, freight movement and public safety. It is important that the roads are kept up to standard so that the roads provide a high level of ride comfort, adequate skid resistance, and acceptable noise levels.

With increasing traffic volumes and ageing infrastructure, early-stage defects can often be overlooked by road inspection crews during manual road inspections. Inspection crews tend to find only defects that have already developed. As traffic demands continue to increase, there is a need for a more efficient technique to find defects in flexible pavements.

When early-stage defects are not identified promptly, minor surface defects can develop into a major structural failure, resulting in significantly higher rehabilitation costs.

This creates an opportunity to automate pavement defect detection using artificial intelligence (AI) and computer vision to identify early surface defects from images and video footage.

1.3 Project Aim

The aim of this project is to develop an artificial intelligence and computer vision-based system able to assist in the detection of visible pavement surface defects in flexible pavements.

1.4 Project Objectives

1.4.1 Objectives of this project

  • To collect video data containing visible surface defects for image-based defect analysis.
  • To extract image frames and create a labelled dataset of visible pavement defects.
  • To train a deep-learning model on surface defects to reliably detect pavement defects under multiple lighting and road conditions.
  • To evaluate model performance using precision, recall, and mAP50, and to record results for analysis.
  • To analyse defects found by the AI and to develop a simplified condition index to support maintenance assessment.
  • To investigate the feasibility of integrating the system into an existing pavement inspection workflow.
  • To achieve a minimum performance target of 0.8 (mAP50) for defect detection across varying environmental conditions.

1.4.2 Constraints of the project

  • Hardware constraints: Training time may be constrained by available hardware performance; Google Colab can be used with usage limits.
  • Time constraint: The project must be finished in the allocated timeframe.
  • Geographic constraint: Data collection will mainly focus on selected roads.
  • Budget constraint: The project will rely on low-cost equipment and publicly available databases.
  • Scope: The project will be finished within the scope of the project.

1.4.3 Limitations of the project

  • Lighting conditions: low light, flat light, shadows, glare and wet surfaces may affect the reliability of the AI.
  • Motion blur: Travelling at high speeds may reduce detection accuracy.
  • GoPro lens: The wide angle may affect bounding box accuracy.
  • iPhone: The iPhone has a high video quality; however, lighting conditions and vehicle speed may decrease image quality. Filming through the windscreen may also add reflections and distortion to the video.
  • False positives/negatives: The model may identify seals, road markings, construction joints and changes in texture.
  • Ride quality: AI will not be able to detect bumps without additional sensors.
  • Surface-only detection: AI can only detect defects that are visible on the surface.
  • Size measurement: AI cannot measure crack depth or rut depth without additional sensors.
  • Overfitting: The model may overfit specific defect types such as potholes, which can reduce its ability to generalise to new road conditions or new roads.
  • Camera vibration: At high speed, vibration may distort the image.
